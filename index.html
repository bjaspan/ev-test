Don't Confuse Them Losing with Us Winning

Democrats are very nervous. The news is all good and they can't believe it. Texas is in play? South Carolina is in play? Kansas is in play? It makes no sense. They are panicking that half the Republicans refuse to be polled and that the polls are way off. Nate Silver ran this cartoon to express Democrats' feelings.




If the Democrats win big tomorrow, many of them are going to draw the wrong conclusion. Morgan Jackson, a senior adviser to North Carolina U.S. Senate candidate Roy Cooper (D), put it this way: "Don't confuse them losing with us winning." What he means is that many Republicans will stay home tomorrow and many independents will vote blue, but the Democratic brand is still toxic in many places. People will be voting that way simply because gas prices are high. This is not a formula for winning in 2028 when the unpopular president will be out of office in a bit over 2 months.

A veteran Democratic pollster who wanted to remain anonymous said: "This is not an affirmation of the Democratic Party in any which way. It is very much a rejection of Trump." Matt Barron, a Democratic strategist in the Midwest, said: "When your opponents are destroying themselves, just get out of the way and let it happen." Lis Smith, an architect of Pete Buttigieg's 2020 campaign, said that Democrats winning in deep-red territory are doing that by distancing themselves from the national party. That is not an option for the 2028 Democratic presidential nominee. Smith also noted that in a wave election, a lot of average-to-mediocre candidates get swept in by the wave. That won't happen next time, absent another wave.

If Democrats don't deliver next year (which they probably won't, because Trump will veto every bill they pass unless it somehow has a billion dollars for him personally in it), in 2028 many people could switch sides again, saying: "We gave you the keys to the kingdom and you didn't deliver for us." This election is shaping up to be an anti-incumbent election, not a pro-Democrats election. Democrats get that wrong at their peril.

It is likely that the DSA candidates and DSA-adjacent candidates will win their races easily and all of a sudden the progressive wing of the Democratic Party will commence saying: "See, this is the way to go in 2028." If Abdul El-Sayed wins big in Michigan, they will be howling this to the moon. That could be true, but one Senate victory against a weak opponent and half a dozen DSA members winning in D+30 House districts does not a realignment make.

However, there is possibly a different story that we might get out of tomorrow. It is possible, not likely, but just possible that Democrats could win Senate seats in North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Mississippi, and Texas—a rout in the Sout'. If that happens, or most of it happens, (Mississippi is really a reach), then the message should be exactly the opposite: Democrats can win in 2028 in the South (!) if they pick a moderate who plays well there. That would be music to the ears of Gov. Andy Beshear (D-KY), who is definitely not DSA-adjacent.

Four other Senate races to watch are those in Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and South Dakota. If moderate Democrats (or moderate Democrats hiding in purple clothing) do well, that also sends the message that Democrats can win traditionally red states, but not with "Defund the police" as their slogan. Victories by both DSA(-adjacent) candidates and moderates in the South and Midwest sends a very mixed message for 2028. For Senate and House races it is straightforward to have a candidate in each contest who is a good fit for the local electorate. That doesn't work in national elections. Democrats have to realize that and think about how best to win in swing and red states. We expect that both moderates and progressives will do well tomorrow, so cue the start of the 2028 battle on Wednesday. (V)

Early Voting Is Surging

According to early-voting export Prof. Michael McDonald, more than 54 million Americans have already voted this year. The pace is similar to the 2018 midterms, when the Democrats flipped 41 House seats. McDonald said that if the pace keeps up, it could exceed the 2018 midterms, which had highest midterm turnout rate since 1914. Early voting is up 30% over 2022.

McDonald said that the turnout is very uneven, with Democrats and independents fully engaged and Republicans much less so. So far, 43% of early voters are Democrats vs. 32% who are Republicans. He also said that young voters and minorities are turning out in large numbers. In fact, he sees very high engagement among Blacks, Latinos, and Asian Americans, but less among whites. He also noted that early voting banks votes that can't change, even if there is some major news today or tomorrow. Nate Silver estimates that at least 40% of Michiganders and Michigeese have already voted, so their votes can't be affected by someone going rogue (see below).

In-person early voting is up more than voting by absentee ballot. This is consistent with more Democrats voting early, with many of them afraid the USPS will intercept and destroy their ballots to please Donald Trump. Voting in person eliminates that threat, even though Trump's threat was to have the USPS seize ballots going to voters, not from voters.

Early voting also varies a lot by state. In Texas, which has hot races for senator and governor, more than 6.3 million had voted as of last Wednesday. That is 50% more than in 2020. Early voting is also up more than 30% compared to 2022 in Kansas, Maine, Michigan, and North Carolina, all of which have Senate races this year. Early voting will taper off now as some states do not allow it up to Election Day. (V)

Court Tells Trump "No" Yet Again

On Sunday, the U.S. District court for the district of Alaska dismissed Donald Trump's suit against the state without leave to amend. In other words, the U.S can't file an amended complaint for the same claims. Judge Sharon Gleason granted the motion brought by the GOP-controlled state government on standing grounds. She held that even if the feds can establish an injury caused by defendants (i.e. that the non-incumbent Dan Sullivan's presence on the ballot violates federal law), there's nothing the Court can do about it. There's no remedy or redress for the feds' complaint. Thus, they don't meet the criteria to get into federal court.

The Alaska Supreme Court already addressed this issue and allowed the other Sullivan to remain on the ballot. And the incumbent Sullivan is identified as such on the ballot. Plus, as we've pointed out many times, Alaska uses ranked choice voting so if anyone is truly confused, they can rank both candidates first and second.

Alaska argued that the DoJ's demand to segregate ballots to determine if voters were confused was impossible. The Judge agreed and held that the requested relief is "infeasible":

As explained by the State and Intervenor-Defendants, many voters have already cast their ballots in the November 2026 general election. Those cast ballots have been commingled and cannot now be segregated or the identity of the person who cast each ballot ascertained. More fundamentally, the United States provides no workable plan as to how it proposes to segregate ballots cast for Daniel J. Sullivan on Election Day and then conduct a court-ordered unspecified 'investigation' of those ballots in a manner that would preserve voter secrecy.

Gleason also wrote that intervention at this late date would create voter confusion and could cause people to stay away from the polls. Under the Purcell principle, courts are to avoid taking any such actions so close to election day.

But Politico points out that the feds seem to have moved on to Plan B, which is to intimidate anyone who seems to support Dan J. Sullivan. Apparently, the FBI has "visited" donors to the retired teacher's campaign. The U.S. Attorney in Alaska has said he's investigating despite the fact that Republicans have engaged in this practice more often and in this cycle and federal law doesn't explicitly prohibit it. The New Campaign Worker Is an AI Bot

While the air war is always important in a campaign, so is the ground war. Voters are much more likely to be convinced to switch sides after talking to a campaign worker who explains what his or her candidate wants and how it will help the voter than by watching a slick TV ad. The trouble is that send ground troops out there to talk to all the voters requires a lot of ground troops that most campaigns don't have.

Enter AI. Candidates can hire specialized AI firms and give them campaign literature to train a chatbot on. This includes candidate speeches, press releases, the candidate's platform and the candidate's website for starters. Then the chatbot sends out text message to voters, asking them to respond by text message. The Democratic firm of Vector Political has had its bots send out 3 million text messages so far. They have gotten 35,000 people to engage in conversations with the bots—without telling the voters they were talking to bots. While the yield is barely over 1%, engaging with 35,000 voters is a lot, especially since it doesn't require any paid workers or volunteers and costs very little. Furthermore, AI can analyze the 35,000 conversations and summarize its findings to the campaign staff. In this way the candidate can get a good idea of what is on the voters' minds. It is almost like running a 35,000-person focus group.

In addition to sucking up data, some AI systems score each person who responds with a score of 0 to 100, with 0 meaning someone who hates the candidate and 100 meaning a potential donor and/or campaign volunteer. This information tells the campaign who to ignore going forward and who to approach and how.

Some of the conversations with the bots have become quite personal in ways they might not have with a campaign worker at the door. It is at least partly analogous to the fact that robopolls have been more accurate than live interviewer polls in some cases because some voters are willing to tell a computer that they are voting for a racist candidate but are not willing to tell a person that, possibly because they fear a person might say "shame on you" whereas they know a computer won't do that.

Some experiments have shown that chatbots can actually get some voters to change their minds. In one experiment the chatbots were 4x more effective at moving voters than TV ads were. Since chatbots are also cheaper than TV ads, expect more of them in the future, for better or worse. (V)

Musk Is Spending Nearly Half a Billion Dollars on the Midterms

After the disastrous Wisconsin Supreme Court election, Elon Musk said he was getting out of politics. But he's pretty fickle and soon got back in—to the tune of $446 million. That is what he has spent this year.

Musk dumped $25 million on the Wisconsin race and his candidate was crushed. But he has so much money, he doesn't even notice losing $25 million or even $446 million. It's just peanuts to him, but in some cases it could flip a race. He has dumped money in 40 House and Senate races of interest to him. Being chronically online himself, Musk has little regard for traditional television advertising. This is how his main personal PAC, America PAC, has spent $216 million. He likes digital ads but oddly enough, also flyers sent to voters. Most of the rest of the money went to the official Republican House and Senate PACs:



So far, more of Musk's money has gone to Texas than any other state. It has been $168 million so far, an enormous amount for one state in a midterm election. Most of that has gone to negative ads about James Talarico. This should give you an idea about the focus:



Look at the ad closely. Talarico is on baseball Team Woke, along with a Black player and a Latino player. And all of the strikes are about gender, especially transgender. That's all Musk has to work with. Talarico works with Black people and Latinos and supports trans people. He's not a Real American like you. That's the entire message. Note that strike two is about men playing on girls' teams, not boys. Maybe someone, somewhere has proposed adult men playing on high school girls' teams, but if so, we missed it. Nothing about how Ken Paxton will lower gas prices or get the Epstein files out there. Nobody would believe that, of course. So this is where campaigns are now.

By making himself the poster child for billionaires buying elections, Musk may be inadvertently helping Democrats get rid of megadonors altogether if they get the trifecta in 2028. They could write a bill banning all campaign spending in excess of the current $3,500 limit per candidate per election and $5,000 limit to the party committees. It would be a three-step process:

  1. Create a carve-out in the Senate rules so bills on campaign finance can't be fillibustered.
  2. Strip the Supreme Court of its appellate jurisdiction on campaign finance cases and make it a felony for a justice to vote on any issue Congress has banned the Court from taking.

Massie Will Force a Vote on Impeaching Hegseth

Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY) will soon be out of a job, but for the moment he is still a representative. Congress has to come back by Dec. 11 to pass a bill to fund the government. Massie has said that as soon as it is back, he will file a privileged motion to impeach Sec. of Defense Pete Hegseth. Assuming the parliamentarian approves his request—and if the parliamentarian refuses, he will probably be fired by Speaker Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) on Jan. 3 if the Democrats take the House—the House must vote on the motion within 2 days. All Democrats will support it and there are probably enough Republicans who are unhappy with Hegseth—for losing a war that has cost $50 billion so far with no end in sight and that will enrich Iran from levying tolls in the future—to get 4 more votes.

What the Senate will do with the impeachment is anyone's guess. If Trump wants to use Hegseth as the fall guy for losing the war, he might instruct Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) to proceed with the trial. Of course, Hegseth could resign to avoid being convicted.

Hegseth has a house in Tennessee and dreamed up the cockamamie plan of being appointed to the Senate seat of soon-to-be elected-Gov. Marsha Blackburn (R-TN). Blackburn seems cool to the idea. Her term is up in 2028, so Hegseth would have to run for a full term then. If North Carolina is lost and South Carolina and Mississippi are even in play, we can imagine that she would want a much stronger candidate in 2028, lest that seat also be in play. Tennessee has eight Republican representatives now, maybe nine in January, so surely she can find one of them who would be a stronger 2028 candidate than Hegseth. Also, Hegseth is widely despised in the Senate, so he would be a terrible senator and have no influence at all in the Senate. We doubt she will play along. There is nothing in it for her or for Tennessee.

Massie's motion has eight articles of impeachment. It argues that Hegseth violated the 1973 War Powers Resolution in Iran, and other U.S. laws in Venezuela, Yemen, and elsewhere. There is also one about trying to suppress the speech of a U.S. senator.

If the trial is in December, with the current Senate, there could be a quick vote and acquittal. If the trial is in January, when Democrats may control the Senate, it could be a real circus. We can't see Hegseth sticking around for that. If Trump doesn't fire him by Jan. 3, he will probably resign rather than face a trial in the Senate. What happens after that remains to be seen. Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ) could file a civil suit against Hegseth for trying to suppress his First Amendment rights and parents of soldiers killed in Iran could too, claiming Hegseth should have refused the illegal orders from Trump to bomb Iran. (V)

A Congress Controlled by Democrats Could Weigh in on Trump's Census Plans

The third clause of Art. I, Sec. 2 of the Constitution begins as follow:

Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States which may be included within this Union, according to their respective Numbers, which shall be determined by adding to the whole Number of free Persons, including those bound to Service for a Term of Years, and excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths of all other Persons.

The actual Enumeration shall be made within three Years after the first Meeting of the Congress of the United States, and within every subsequent Term of ten Years, in such Manner as they shall by Law direct.

The Fourteenth Amendment also addresses apportionment. Sec. 2 begins as follows.

Representatives shall be apportioned among the several States according to their respective numbers, counting the whole number of persons in each State, excluding Indians not taxed.

Note that both refers to persons, not citizens. Donald Trump wants to change that and have the census count only citizens (and if he is forced to, green card holders). But there are many noncitizen persons in the U.S. who are legal (holders of H-1B visas, foreign students, businessmen, refugees, asylum seekers, and others) And of course, there are persons in the U.S. illegally. All of these noncitizens have always been counted in the past because the Constitution says persons. The founders knew the difference between an inhabitant and a citizen and used the word "citizen" when they meant citizen, for example, members of Congress must be citizens and the president must be a natural-born citizen. It has never been tested if a baby delivered by a Caesarian section is considered natural born. Shakespeare, for one, didn't think so.

The change that Trump wants would force a dramatic shift of political power from blue states (which, by and large, have the most noncitizens) to red states by moving House seats and electoral votes from blue states to red states. Over 300 federal programs allocate funds based on population, as determined by the census. So Trump wants to shift power and money to the red states. He also wants to eliminate the questions abut race and ethnicity from the census, to make minorities less visible. These changes are a real threat to democracy.

The 2030 census is scheduled to happen on April 1, 2030. All persons in the U.S. on that date should be enumerated. Plans and forms for it are being formulated already. If Democrats capture Congress on Tuesday, they could try to block Trump's plans. Congress doesn't have direct authority over the census; Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick does. However, it could pass laws specifically stating that all noncitizens, legal or otherwise, must be included in the count or it is not valid and that there must be questions about race and ethnicity.

There is no way on God's green earth that a stand-alone bill with these directions could survive a Senate filibuster. However, Democrats could add these provisions to a must-pass bill, like the budget or the defense appropriations bill by simple majority vote and dare the Republicans to torpedo the bills, with all the publicity and complications that would bring. We have been down that road before. In 2024, Sen. Bill Hagerty (R-TN) made an attempt to add an amendment to an omnibus spending bill to specifically exclude noncitizens from the census but the votes weren't there.

Sen. Andy Kim (D-NJ) is the ranking member on the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs census subcommittee, and he is on top of this. He recently said: "The census is enshrined in our Constitution and not up for political debate." If the Democrats take the Senate, he will become chairman of the subcommittee and use his power to push back on Trump. Also incensed by this proposal is Rep. Robert Garcia (D-CA), who is very likely to become chairman of the House Oversight Committee, which has broad powers to investigate almost anything the chairman wants to investigate.

If Congress doesn't overrule Trump's plans by laws, there will surely be a lawsuit and will be up to the court to determine what a person is. (V)
